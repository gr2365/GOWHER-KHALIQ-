1. What is Geology?

Geology is the study of Earth: its materials, structure, processes and history. Geologists use observations of rocks, minerals, landforms and geophysical data to understand how Earth has changed over time.

Mineralogy
Minerals, their properties and formation.
Petrology
Rocks, their origins and transformations.
Structural geology
Deformation, folds, faults and rock structures.
Stratigraphy & palaeontology
Rock layers, their sequence and the fossil record.

Geology connects with geography, chemistry, physics, biology and environmental science. It is also important for understanding natural hazards, groundwater, soils and Earth resources.

2. Earth's Interior

Earth's internal layers are inferred from evidence including earthquake waves, gravity, magnetic measurements and laboratory experiments. Depths below are approximate.

LayerApproximate depthMain features
CrustSurface to about 5–70 kmThin, solid outer layer. Oceanic crust is generally thinner and denser than continental crust.
MantleTo about 2,900 kmMostly solid silicate rock that can deform and flow very slowly over geological time.
Outer coreAbout 2,900–5,150 kmLiquid iron-rich metal; its motion helps generate Earth's magnetic field.
Inner coreAbout 5,150 km to Earth's centre (~6,371 km)Solid iron-rich metal under immense pressure.

Important distinction: The crust is not the same as the lithosphere. The lithosphere includes the crust and the rigid uppermost mantle; it is broken into tectonic plates. Beneath it, parts of the upper mantle are hot and weak enough to deform slowly.

3. Minerals

A mineral is a naturally occurring substance, usually inorganic, with an ordered crystalline structure and a characteristic chemical composition that may vary within defined limits. A rock is an aggregate of one or more minerals or mineraloids.

How geologists identify minerals

  • Hardness: resistance to scratching, often compared using the Mohs scale. It is a relative ranking, not a linear measurement.
  • Streak: colour of a mineral's powder, tested on an unglazed porcelain plate.
  • Lustre: how a surface reflects light, such as metallic or glassy.
  • Cleavage and fracture: how a mineral breaks along planes of weakness or irregularly.
  • Density, crystal form and reaction: additional clues; no single property is always enough to identify a mineral.

Examples: quartz (silicate), calcite (carbonate), halite (halide) and hematite (iron oxide). A mineral's colour alone can be misleading because impurities may change it.

4. The Three Main Rock Groups

The rock cycle describes how rocks can change from one group to another through melting, cooling, weathering, burial, heat and pressure. It is a cycle of possible pathways, not a single fixed sequence.

GroupFormationExamples and clues
IgneousMagma or lava cools and solidifies.Granite cools slowly underground and often has visible crystals; basalt cools at or near the surface and is commonly fine-grained.
SedimentaryDeposited particles may compact and cement; other sedimentary rocks form by chemical precipitation or biological accumulation.Sandstone, shale and limestone. Bedding and fossils may be present.
MetamorphicExisting rock changes through heat, pressure and chemically active fluids without completely melting.Slate, schist, gneiss and marble. Some metamorphic rocks show foliation; others do not.

Weathering and erosion

Weathering breaks down or alters rock at or near Earth's surface. It may be physical, chemical or biological. Erosion removes and transports material by water, wind, ice or gravity. Deposition occurs when transported material settles.

5. Plate Tectonics

Plate tectonics explains the movement of Earth's lithospheric plates and many patterns of earthquakes, volcanoes, mountain building and ocean-basin development. Plate motion is driven by forces including slab pull and ridge push, within a slowly convecting mantle system.

Divergent boundary
Plates move apart. New oceanic crust can form at spreading ridges; continental rifting may begin here.
Convergent boundary
Plates move together. Subduction can form trenches and volcanic arcs; continent-continent collision can build high mountains.
Transform boundary
Plates slide past each other. Motion is commonly accommodated by faults and may produce earthquakes.

Volcanoes and earthquakes are common at many plate boundaries, but not every volcano lies on a boundary: some form above mantle hotspots. Earthquake magnitude describes the size of an earthquake using a calculated measure; intensity describes observed effects at a particular place.

6. Geological Time and Dating

Earth is about 4.54 billion years old. Geologists use relative dating to determine whether rocks or events are older or younger, and radiometric dating to estimate numerical ages from radioactive decay in suitable minerals or rocks.

Useful relative-dating principles

  • Superposition: in an undisturbed sequence of sedimentary layers, lower layers are generally older than layers above them.
  • Original horizontality: sedimentary layers are generally deposited close to horizontal; tilting happened later.
  • Cross-cutting relationships: a fault or intrusion that cuts rock is younger than the rock it cuts.
  • Inclusions: fragments enclosed in a rock are generally older than the rock surrounding them.
  • Fossil succession: fossil assemblages occur in a recognizable order through rock layers and help correlate strata.

The geological time scale divides Earth's history into eons, eras, periods and smaller units. Boundaries are based on major changes in rocks, fossils and Earth's history; many are formally defined using international standards.
